1. Economic Example: Personal Savings Rate Over Time

Imagine you are a data analyst working for a small team within the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA). Your group monitors long‑term financial trends to help economists and policy makers understand how Americans save money over time. One of the most important indicators your team studies is the personal savings rate—the percentage of personal income that individuals save rather than spend.

Your supervisor tells you:

“We need a clear visual showing how the U.S. personal savings rate has changed over the years. Something intuitive, something that shows flow over time. Use one of the standard datasets we keep for training—look for the economics dataset inside the ggplot2 package.”

You lookup the data and confirm that economics is a built‑in dataset that ships automatically with ggplot2. It contains monthly U.S. economic indicators collected from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (FRED), including:

  • date — monthly date values

  • psavert — personal savings rate (%)

  • pce, unemploy, uempmed, etc. (other variables you won’t need right now)

For your first task, your supervisor wants something simple but polished:

Your Mission

Create a filled area chart showing how the personal savings rate (psavert) changes over time (date).

Specifically, you are expected to:

  1. Use geom_area() to draw the filled shape beneath the curve.

  2. Make sure the plot has clear axis labels and an informative title.

  3. Use a fill color with soft transparency so the shape feels like “economic volume” changing through time.

  4. Produce a figure that one could show to a policymaker and expect them to understand at a glance.

✔️ Expected Output (description)

Students should produce a plot that shows:

  • The x‑axis: dates from the late 1960s to present

  • The y‑axis: % personal savings rate

  • A filled area under the curve

  • A gentle color and light transparency

BONOS: add a horizontal dashed line at the median savings rate to provide a reference point for viewers.

  • An overall impression of long‑term trends in U.S. saving behavior

The final result should look like a smooth “mountain range” of savings over time—simple, readable, and ready for interpretation.

2. Biological Example: Chick Weight Distributions by Diet

Imagine you are a young researcher in an animal physiology lab studying how diet influences growth in juvenile chickens. The lab ran a controlled experiment where newly hatched chicks were randomly assigned to four different diets. Each chick was weighed repeatedly as it grew. Your supervisor tells you:

“Before we analyze individual growth curves, let’s get a quick sense of the overall weight distribution for each diet. Use density curves — they’re smooth, intuitive, and perfect for comparing population‑level differences.”

You open the R datasets and find the built‑in ChickWeight data frame in the dataset package. It contains:

Your Mission

  • weight: body weight in grams

  • Time: age (days since birth)

  • Chick: individual ID

  • Diet — diet treatment (1, 2, 3, or 4)

Your Mission

Since weight is a continuous variable and Diet is categorical, this is an ideal biological dataset for density plots. Your supervisor wants you to produce a visualization that:

Compares weight distributions between diets. Uses overlaid density curves, each with a different color. Uses transparency so overlapping regions remain visible. Helps determine whether certain diets tend to produce heavier or lighter chicks.

  • Generate a density plot where the area is not filled:

✔️ Expected Output (description)

Each diet has a different colored curve. - All curves are overlaid on the same axes.

- X‑axis = chick weight

- Y‑axis = density

- Plot shows how the overall weight distribution differs across diets (e.g., Diet 3 tends to produce heavier chicks).

The final plot should visually communicate growth differences without requiring statistical modeling.

Story setup

You’re assisting in a nutrition/physiology lab testing whether vitamin C delivery method changes tooth growth in guinea pigs. Animals received vitamin C as either orange juice (OJ) or ascorbic acid (VC) at different doses. You want a quick, smooth comparison of tooth length (len) across supplement types to see if one tends to yield longer teeth.

What students should produce that generates: DO ONE OF THESE ALTERNATIVES

✔️ Expected Output (description)

Built-in biological dataset

data(“ToothGrowth”)

Variables:

- len = tooth length (numeric, continuous)

- supp = supplement type (“OJ” orange juice, “VC” ascorbic acid)

- dose = dose level (0.5, 1, 2 mg)

  • An overlaid density plot of len by supp, using kernel = “gaussian”.
